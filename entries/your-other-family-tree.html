You can trace your ancestry back through parents and grandparents until the names run out. Behind that tree sits a second one, made of inventions. Each era below pairs one invention with a famous family, counts that family's known descendants, and estimates how many of them exist because the invention cut the number of children who died. The estimates are rough: they apply population-wide death rates to one family, so treat them as order-of-magnitude.

Around 1800, about 1 billion people were alive. Today it's over 8 billion.

Farming and the family of Confucius

Confucius (551 to 479 BC) lived in a farming state in eastern China. His family keeps the largest genealogy in the world. Its 1937 edition listed 560,000 members. The 2009 edition covers 83 generations and over 2 million descendants.

Every one of them descends from people fed by planted fields. Hunter-gatherer bands couldn't support anything like that density.

Descendants enabled: effectively all 2 million.

The heavy plough and Charlemagne

Charlemagne ruled much of western Europe around 800 AD, as the heavy mouldboard plough and three-field rotation spread north. The plough let farmers drain and work heavy clay soil. Three-field rotation cut idle land from half the fields to a third. Economists studying the plough estimate it caused about 10% of the rise in population density in the High Middle Ages, and over 40% of the productivity gain in clay-heavy Denmark.

Statisticians estimate that nearly everyone of European descent alive today descends from Charlemagne, which is hundreds of millions of people.

Descendants enabled: if about 10% of medieval population growth came from the plough, roughly 1 in 10 of Charlemagne's medieval descendants owed their existence to it. Those lines compound, so the share among his living descendants is likely higher.

Inoculation and the Romanovs

In 1768, Catherine the Great had herself and her son Paul inoculated against smallpox. In mid-18th-century Britain, smallpox caused about a third of all deaths among children aged 1 to 4. Over 80% of infected children died.

Descendants enabled: if smallpox would otherwise have killed about 1 child in 8, roughly 1 of Paul's children, 4 grandchildren, and 9 great-grandchildren survived because of inoculation. Each of them had families of their own, so the effect multiplies down the tree.

Clean water and the Lincolns

Abraham Lincoln had four sons. Eddie died at 3, Willie at 11 of what was most likely typhoid, a waterborne disease, and Tad at 18. Only Robert lived into middle age, and Lincoln's direct line ended in 1985.

A generation later, cities filtered and chlorinated their water. Economists David Cutler and Grant Miller found that filtration cut typhoid deaths by 46%, infant deaths by 43%, and deaths among children aged 1 to 4 by 46% in major US cities.

Descendants enabled: with those rates, a family like the Lincolns would expect to keep about one more son. For the Lincolns, that's the difference between a line that ended and one that might still be going.

Bread from air and the 20th-century family

By 1900, farms were running low on nitrogen fertiliser. Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch worked out how to pull nitrogen from the air, and industrial production began in 1913. Haber also led Germany's poison gas programme in the First World War.

A 2008 study in Nature Geoscience by Jan Willem Erisman and colleagues estimated that Haber-Bosch fertiliser supported 48% of the world's population that year, roughly 4 billion people. Queen Elizabeth II's generation was the first to grow up with it.

Descendants enabled: for a typical family alive today, about half its members. In a family of 10 cousins, siblings, and children, that's roughly 5 people.

Penicillin and Anne Miller

In March 1942, Anne Sheafe Miller lay near death in New Haven Hospital with a streptococcal infection after a miscarriage. Sulfa drugs, transfusions, and surgery had failed. Doctors John Bumstead and Orvan Hess gave her penicillin, and she became the first patient in the US saved by it. She died in 1999 at 90.

She left three sons, six grandchildren, and seven great-grandchildren.

Descendants enabled: all 16, plus everyone born into her family since 1999.

Shorter wheat and the families of India

In 1968, biologist Paul Ehrlich predicted that hundreds of millions would starve in the 1970s. In India, geneticist M. S. Swaminathan persuaded the government in 1966 to buy 18,000 tonnes of Norman Borlaug's short-stalked Mexican wheat seed. Wheat production rose from 12 million tonnes in 1965 to 26.4 million tonnes in 1971 to 1972, more than doubling in about six years. Borlaug later wrote that much of the credit belonged to Swaminathan. India's population has since grown from about 500 million to over 1.4 billion.

Descendants enabled: no single famous family fits here, so the family is a region. In Punjab, the first state to plant the new wheat, the extra grain fed families that were one bad harvest from famine. A large share of the roughly 900 million people India has added since 1970 owe their existence to that doubled harvest, though no study puts a precise figure on it.

The family resemblance

Almost none of these inventions are machines you'd put in a museum. They're about food, water, and children surviving to have children. Your family tree tells you who you came from. This one tells you why so many of them made it.
